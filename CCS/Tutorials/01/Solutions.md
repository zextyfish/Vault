Exercise 1: Molecular Dynamics Simulations
   a. What is the central idea behind molecular dynamics simulations?
   ${}\to{}$ The central idea behind MD is to use computers to model the physical movements and interactions of atoms and molecules over time. This is similar to real life experiments but on a computer system where we produce the trajectories of every single particle.
   A real experiment proceeds as follows.
   - A sample of material to be studied is prepared.
   - A measurement instrument is connected and properties of interest are studied over certain time interval. For most measurement, to reduce statistical noise long time averages are used.
   In a Molecular Dynamics simulation,  same approach is followed. 
   - Sample Preparation: A model system is selected with ${}N{}$ particles
   - Equilibration: solve Newton’s equations of motion for this system until the properties of the system no longer change with time
   - Measurements
   
   b. How is a molecular dynamics simulation usually carried out? Are the simulations usually divided into different parts? What is the purpose of each part?
   ${}\to{}$ Molecular Dynamics simulations are usually carried out as follows:
   - Initialize the system: select initial positions and velocities
   - Forces on all particles are computed.
   - Newton’s equations of motion are Integrated. This step and the previous one make up the core of the simulation. They are repeated until we have computed the time evolution of the system for the desired length of time.
   - After completion of the central loop, the averages of measured quantities are computed
   
   c. What is a thermostat? Is a thermostat compatible with your answer to the first question
   ${}\to$ In molecular dynamics (MD), a thermostat is a computational algorithm used
   to control and maintain a constant temperature in a simulated system.
   Because temperature is directly proportional to the average kinetic energy of the atoms (equipartition theorem), a thermostat monitors this kinetic energy and adjusts it when necessary to keep the system at a desired target temperature. Examples: Andersen, Nose-Hoover, Langevin, and Berendsen thermostats.
   Yes, a thermostat is fully compatible with the core principles of molecular dynamics. For simulating a ${}NVT{}$ Ensemble, thermostat can be used to simulate the real world situation. It supplements the equation of motions to simulate a system connected to external "heat bath".
   A Thermostat adds to make MD simulation more "real".
   
   d. Does a trajectory generated by a molecular dynamics simulation predict the evolution of a real system?
   ${}\to{}$ Molecular Dynamics simulation does not predict the exact evolution of a real system. It can at best be treated as probabilistic evolution of a system.
   Limitations:
   - Force Field Approximations: Force fields are mathematical simplifications of quantum mechanics. It doesn't accurately capture a specific chemical interaction, the resulting trajectory drifts from real system.
   - Sensitivity: These simulations are really sensitive to initial parameters.
   
2. Exercise 2: Langevin and Brownian Dynamics
   Given Langevin equation given:
   $$
   m \frac{\text{d}v(t)}{\text{d}t} = - \gamma v(t) + \xi(t) \label{eq:langevin}
   $$
   where ${}\xi(t){}$ is Gaussian noise:
   $$
   \langle \xi(t) \rangle = 0  \hspace{3cm} \langle \xi(t)\xi(t^\prime)\rangle =2\gamma k_{\text{B}}T\delta(t-t^\prime) \label{eq:noise}
   $$
   The Brownian dynamics are described by:
   $$
    \frac{\text{d}x(t)}{\text{d}t}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\xi(t) \label{eq:brownian}
   $$
   In Eq. ${}\eqref{eq:langevin}{}$  the left hand side term represents inertia and on right hand side there are two parts a deterministic part (${}-\gamma v(t){}$), which depends on the velocity of the particle, and second non-deterministic part (${}\xi(t){}$), which comes from Gaussian distribution.
   The deterministic part models the friction offered by a liquid on a moving particle (Stokes' law) while the non-deterministic part models the random collision happening as it depends on the temperature ${}T{}$.
   Eq.${}\eqref{eq:langevin}$ can be rewritten as
   $$
   \frac{\text{d}v(t)}{\text{d}t}=- \frac{\gamma}{m}v(t)+\frac{\xi(t)}{m}
   $$
   In Brownian motion (Overdamped limit), we assume the inertia is negligible. Thus,
   $$
   \frac{\text{d}v(t)}{\text{d}t}\approx 0
   $$
   This gives us
   $$
   0 = -\gamma v(t) + \xi(t)
   $$
   Rearranging gives:
   $$
   v(t)=\frac{1}{\gamma}\xi(t)
   $$
   $$\boxed {\frac{\text{d}x(t)}{\text{d}t}=\frac{1}{\gamma } \xi(t)}$$
